# Writing styles

How to write course text: whose style comes first, what holds in every style, and the styles to choose from, with when each fits. Read it before writing or rewriting more than a sentence or two, and in a language review. Writing a new page or section that teaches: follow `course-material/references/writing/new-material.md` too; this file alone isn't enough for it.

## Whose style comes first

1. What the teacher says now.
2. The course's section in `preferences.md` and `course-rules.md`, then their general lines.
3. The course's own style, inferred from its text and from how the teacher writes: terms, spelling variety, typography, form of address, register, voice. Never ask the teacher to choose what the course already shows.
4. The style below that fits the text.

Saving stated preferences, never restyling unasked, and mentioning resolved conflicts: root `SKILL.md` **Remembering** and **Writing style**.

## In every style

- Clear: a student understands it on first reading.
- Accurate and precise. Claims follow `course-material/references/guidance/honesty.md`.
- Concise, never flattened: cut padding, filler, hedging and repetition, and say each thing once. Keep precise terms (explained on first use), nuance, depth and the teacher's voice. Never invent length limits.
- One term per concept, as the course already uses it.
- Logical progression, relevant examples, and activities aligned with the objectives.
- Accessible: `course-material/references/accessibility.md` for images, headings, links, tables and media.

## Common AI style mistakes

Habits AI-written drafts often fall into. They lower the quality of the text: words without content, a flattened voice, reasoning hidden in structure. Avoid them in text you draft. In a review, never call a teacher's or student's text AI-written; name the concrete problem (padding, hype, a list that hides the reasoning).
- Stock words as filler or praise, not where the word is the field's own term (robust statistics, a test harness, a fitness landscape): delve, dive into, crucial, pivotal, seamless, leverage, unlock, tapestry, realm, "it's important to note", "in today's world", "whether you're a beginner or…", "let's explore". Finnish calques: "on tärkeää huomata", "sukelletaan". [Kobak-2025] [Wikipedia-AI-signs]
- Hype and cheerleading: "exciting", "amazing", "you've got this", exclamation marks, emoji.
- Structure standing in for reasoning: bullet lists where the ideas depend on each other, bold scattered for emphasis, a heading over every two sentences, the same template in every section.
- Empty frames: openers that announce what follows, recaps that repeat the page, closing pep talk.
- Devices used as a habit or for rhythm rather than to carry a point: "not X, but Y", lists of exactly three, rhetorical questions the text then answers, colon-then-reveal sentences, frequent dash asides. A question that makes the student think, or a contrast that corrects a misconception, stays.
- Hedging stacks ("can often potentially help") and vague praise of the subject.

Fix by saying the thing directly and keeping the reasoning in sentences.

## Styles

**Explanatory**, like a good non-fiction book or textbook in the field: a voice, direct address where the course uses it, a line of reasoning across paragraphs, examples near abstractions, questions that make the student think. In practice:
- Usually a concrete case first, then the general idea, stated explicitly; definitions, proofs and reference sections can start from the general.
- Each paragraph makes one point and moves the reasoning on; the link to the previous one is stated (because, so, but, for example), not left to headings or bullets.
- Terms are defined in the sentence where they first matter, then used the same way every time.
- Sentences vary in length; verbs are precise; the subject of a sentence is the thing being explained.
- It anticipates where a student goes wrong and says so at that point.
- Examples come from the course's own running examples or the students' world, not generic placeholders.
- Use for: subject content, the bulk of most courses.
- Not for: course information and instructions, where students need to find and act, not follow an argument.

**Instructional**, like clear official text: what to do, by when, and what counts, in the order the student needs it; dates, numbers and conditions exact.
- Use for: course information, how to take the course, deadlines, grading, exercise instructions, announcements.
- Not for: explanations. Its impersonal register makes them flat; only its clarity carries over.

**Exercise text**: one reading only, every condition stated, no hints the task doesn't intend. Feedback says what was right or wrong and why, in the course's form of address. Detail in `course-material/references/guidance/exercises.md`.
- Use for: questions, answer options, model answers, feedback.
- Not for: rewriting a running exercise's meaning; that is the teacher's call.

**Plain language** (ISO 24495-1): not a separate register but a check on any text. Readers get what they need, find it, understand it and can use it.
- Use as: the frame of the language review's understandability check, in every style. Relevance and findability weigh most for instructional text, understandability for explanations, usability for instructions and exercises.
- Not for: flattening explanations, or replacing the field's terms with everyday words. Agents know the standard; teachers can't read it free, so findings cite the course language's file, not ISO. Never claim conformance. [ISO-2023] [SFS-2023]

**Academic**: dense, formal prose, as in source readings or advanced material the teacher wrote that way on purpose.
- Use for: text that is already academic, kept so; quotations.
- Not for: new explanations unless the course uses it. Only check findability there; don't simplify it.

**Easy-to-read** (selkokieli, lättläst): a separate version adapted for readers who find the standard language hard.
- Use for: only an easy-to-read version the teacher asks for, or a course written for those readers. Finnish: the **Selkokieli** section of `course-material/references/writing/finnish.md`.
- Not for: ordinary course text, even "to make it clearer", or an unasked simplify pass.

## Language files

Read the file for the text's language when writing or reviewing it; other languages follow the course's own conventions. Finnish `course-material/references/writing/finnish.md`, Swedish `course-material/references/writing/swedish.md`, English `course-material/references/writing/english.md`.
