# Writing new teaching material

How to draft a new page or section that teaches something. Read it before writing one; `course-material/references/writing/styles.md` still decides the style. The page should read like the course's best page, not like a reference card.

## Read first

- Always: `course-material/references/writing/styles.md` (**Explanatory**, **Common AI style mistakes**), the course language's file, and the What to check points of `course-material/references/guidance/learning.md`.
- The subject's guidance where there is one: `course-material/references/guidance/computing.md` for programming, computer science and mathematics; `course-material/references/guidance/knowledge-and-method.md` and `course-material/references/guidance/evidence.md` for science and method; `course-material/references/guidance/reasoning-errors.md` for arguments and statistics.
- When the page needs them: `course-material/references/guidance/honesty.md` for claims, `course-material/references/accessibility.md` for images, tables and code, `course-material/references/guidance/engagement.md` for motivation, `course-material/references/guidance/exercises.md` before suggesting exercises.

## Before writing

- Read the pages before and after where it goes, and the chapter front page. Note what students already know, the terms and examples the course uses, and what comes later. Keep the page design the course uses, for example problem first, then instruction.
- When the page makes claims that need sources, research them first with the `course-material-research` skill.
- Decide what a student can do after the page, and the mistakes beginners commonly make with it. Ask the teacher only when the course doesn't show the level or scope.

## Each new idea

Cover these, in this order by default; leave out a step that has nothing real to say:

1. **Why**: the problem it solves, ideally one the student has just met in the course.
2. **How**: the idea in plain words with a small example that uses only what the course has taught or this page explains, then the general idea stated explicitly.
3. **What happens**: what the example prints, returns or shows, so students can check themselves.
4. **Where beginners go wrong**: a common mistake or error message and how to recognise it, when there is one.

Build each idea on the previous one. Introduce a term or notation where it is first needed, including words like function, method or type; don't lean on one the course hasn't taught.

## Let students do something

- When the course has a block for it, add a short self-check: predict the output, spot the bug, or try a variation. Keep its answer out of sight of the question: in a block that hides it, or after the next section. Otherwise suggest one to the teacher.
- When exercises would help and you can't create them, suggest one or two, with what each practises.

## Leave out

- Code, variables or examples the text never uses.
- Everything in `course-material/references/writing/styles.md` **Common AI style mistakes**.

## Before staging

Read the draft as a student who has read only the earlier pages: every term is explained before it is used, each section answers why as well as how, and each stated result is right. Check examples by reading them; run them only after the teacher's yes, and say which results you didn't run. Then go through **Common AI style mistakes**. Fix what fails, then stage.
