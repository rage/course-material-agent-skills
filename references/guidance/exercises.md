# Exercises and assessment

What exercises can show about students, and how to judge whether they measure what they're meant to. Read it when checking exercises, peer review questions or grading rules, or when the teacher asks about assessment design. How question and feedback text should read: `course-material/references/writing/styles.md` **Exercise text**.

## What to check

- **Don't suggest dropping recall questions** for being simple. Factual recall and reading checks test foundations, give retrieval practice, reveal gaps and prepare for harder tasks. [Yang-2021] [Adesope-2017]
- **Recall read as more than it shows**: a course that treats a reading quiz as proof students read the page, or a recall score as proof of mastery. A correct answer shows knowledge, not where it came from; suggest wording the purpose accordingly. [Kane-2013] [Standards-2014]
- **Exercise type vs objective**: check that each exercise asks for the capability its objective names. Defining a term shows factual knowledge; explaining shows understanding; tracing shows an execution model; implementing shows procedural skill; debugging shows diagnosis; critiquing a study shows evidence evaluation; an unfamiliar problem shows transfer. Report exercises whose type can't show what the objective claims. [Biggs-1996] (editorial)
- **Task wording caps the level**: a task whose cognitive demand is lower than its objective or its highest criterion, so even a strong answer can't show the level the criteria reward. Suggest a task that requires the objective's process, such as judging between two given solutions and saying why. See also **Exercise type vs objective**. [Biggs-1996]
- **Criteria hidden until after the work**: a graded or peer-reviewed task whose criteria students see only after submitting, or not at all. Suggest showing the scale and level descriptions with the task. Rubrics raised performance moderately on average (g ≈ 0.45, 21 studies); effects on self-regulation and self-efficacy were small and uncertain. [Panadero-2023] Peers, students and the teacher assess against the same criteria. (editorial)
- **Quotas**: don't suggest a fixed share of recall, application or reasoning tasks. A terminology-heavy chapter may rightly have many recall questions; a capstone project mostly application. (editorial)
- **Missing parts of the ability (construct underrepresentation)**: a chapter or exam that claims to assess a skill but covers only a narrow slice, such as only syntax questions for "programming competence". Name what's missing. [Messick-1995] [Standards-2014]
- **Irrelevant difficulty (construct-irrelevant variance)**: success that depends on something other than the target: convoluted wording, unfamiliar interface conventions, typing speed, cultural knowledge unrelated to the topic, tight time limits, outside help. Suggest the change that removes it. [Messick-1995] [Standards-2014]
- **Ambiguity and inconsistent scoring**: questions with more than one defensible answer, model solutions that disagree with the instructions, rubric lines a peer reviewer couldn't apply the same way twice. Suggest the clarification. [Standards-2014]
- **Fairness**: barriers unrelated to the objective: inaccessible formats, language harder than the content needs, accommodations applied unevenly. Fairness doesn't mean identical tasks for everyone; it means comparable evidence about the same competence. [Standards-2014]
- **Authentic but uninformative**: a realistic task (team project, open-ended build) used to grade individual competence it can't isolate. Suggest what would show each student's own contribution, or a narrower claim. [Kane-2013] (editorial)
- **Independent vs tool-assisted competence**: neither the exercise nor the course says whether students may use AI or other tools, or whose grade claims independent skill while tools were allowed. Suggest stating which one it measures; both can be legitimate objectives, and the result means different things. A named level per task, on a scale such as the original 2024 version of the AI Assessment Scale, from no AI to full AI use, tells students before they start. [Messick-1995] [Perkins-2024] (editorial)
- **Graded work a chatbot can produce**: a points-bearing open task, such as a general essay or an explanation of course content, that a chatbot answers well. Suggest a task whose product shows the student's own judgement: applying ideas to a given case, justifying a choice, checking and correcting AI output. See also **Independent vs tool-assisted competence**. In one experiment (ChatGPT 3.5, one topic), students who researched with an LLM felt less load but argued worse than students using a search engine. [Stadler-2024] [HYPE-2026] Or give the task a lower weight. (editorial)
- **Effects of the assessment itself**: grading rules that push students towards guessing, copying or skipping practice (e.g. unlimited attempts on a points-bearing quiz with instant answers). Point out the likely behaviour; positive effects alone don't make an assessment valid. [Messick-1995] [Standards-2014]
- **Formative use**: exercises whose results students can't act on (no feedback, no retry, no pointer back to the material). Suggest the smallest addition that lets them improve. [Black-1998]

## Background

### Validity belongs to interpretations

Validity is not a property of an exercise. It's how well evidence supports a particular interpretation or use of its results. Passing an automated test suite supports "the program behaved correctly on these tests", not "the student understands the algorithm" or "the student wrote it alone". Bolder claims need more evidence. [Kane-2013] [Standards-2014]

Before judging an important assessment, answer:

1. What exactly do we want to conclude about the student?
2. What observable performance would be evidence for it?
3. What else could produce the same result?

[Kane-2013]

### Rigor scaled to stakes

A low-stakes diagnostic quiz needs much less precision than a certifying exam. For assessments that decide grades or credits, check: the target competence is defined; tasks cover it; scoring criteria are clear; common wrong answers are anticipated; ambiguous items have been tried out where feasible; accessibility and fairness are reviewed; scorers agree; and the results support the decision they're used for. [Standards-2014]
